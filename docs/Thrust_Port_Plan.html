Thrust on the PicoComputer 3

A phased plan for bringing Jeremy Smith's 1986 BBC Micro Thrust to the PC3 in MMBasic, built on the frame budget and sprite pipeline proven by Bas/chuckie.bas, the new PLAY BBC SOUND engine, and Kieran Connell's annotated disassembly.

The argument

Everything that made Thrust hard on a 6502 is something we can delete

Thrust is a small game with one big idea. The physics is a page of arithmetic on about a dozen variables. The terrain is two numbers per scanline. There are at most 32 objects and 32 particles in the world at once. What made it a technical showpiece in 1986 — XOR plotting, collision detection by self-modifying code, a 256-byte circular terrain window, Q7.8 fixed point — was all workaround for 32K of RAM and a 2 MHz CPU, and none of it needs to survive the port.

What does need to survive is the feel, and the feel is a table of constants. The thrust divisors, the two drag ratios, the tick gating, the rotation rate limit, the 32-entry elliptical angle tables: copy those exactly and the ship flies like the original whether the arithmetic underneath is fixed point or MMBasic floats. Per the house rule that interpreting dominates and the arithmetic is free, floats cost us nothing, so there is no argument for reproducing the fixed point.

The one question that could have killed it

Can MMBasic redraw a screenful of cave wall inside a 33 ms frame? That has been answered before writing a line of BASIC — see the measurement. Because the terrain is stored as long runs at a constant X, a typical frame merges down to 13 to 56 rectangle fills, and the worst frame in all six levels is 158. Not 240 scanline draws. The design is comfortable.

The reference point that matters: Chuckie Egg holds 30 fps with nine moving entities, a full-screen framebuffer copy every frame, and a HUD redraw. Thrust asks for more drawing and less logic.

The original

Four subsystems, all documented

Kieran Connell's disassembly ships three technical references alongside the annotated 6502. They are unusually good — the physics one in particular reads like a design document rather than a transcription. Everything below is from them.

Physics — the midpoint

All forces act on the midpoint, the centre of mass of the ship-and-pod system; ship and pod are derived by offsetting from it along the tether vector in opposite directions. With no pod, the midpoint is the ship. The tether is a damped pendulum driven purely by the tangential component of thrust — there is no gravity torque term, which is correct, because gravity accelerates both masses equally.

32 angles (11.25°). Rotation on 3 frames in 4. Forces on 6 ticks in 16. Thrust ÷16, or ÷32 with the pod. X drag 63/64, Y drag 255/256, tether damping 63/64.

Terrain — two numbers per scanline

For every scanline in the world the game stores the X of the left wall and the X of the right wall; the cave is the gap between. Both are RLE delta-compressed as (count, signed increment) pairs, decoded forwards and backwards so the 256-byte window can be extended at whichever edge the camera moves toward.

Six levels. The largest is 1262 scanlines deep. All six terrains together are under 250 bytes of source data.

Sprites — XOR everything

Every sprite is plotted with EOR, so erasing is redrawing. Collision falls out for free: after XOR-erasing a pixel the result should be zero, and anything non-zero means something else drew over it. The test site is three bytes of self-modifying code patched between AND mask,X and JMP.

17 ship shapes cover 32 headings; the other 15 are horizontal mirrors. Plus a shield sprite.

Sound — four envelopes, nine blocks

Four OSWORD &08 envelopes and nine OSWORD &07 parameter blocks, sitting in the source as raw bytes. The explosion is the good one: a tone on channel 1 and noise pitch 7 on channel 0, which clocks the noise from channel 1's pitch so the bang is tuned rather than a hiss.

Lines 10786-10840 of thrust.6502.

One transcription check to do before trusting the sound data: envelope 1, 3 and 4 are 14 bytes each in the listing, as an ENVELOPE block should be, but envelope 2 groups to 13. Read it out of the binary rather than the source.

Where the disassembly's notes are wrong

The three reference documents are the best thing about that repository and they are what made this port cheap, but they are notes rather than a specification, and two claims in ship-physics.md do not match the 6502 beside them. Both were caught by reading the assembly when the behaviour looked odd, and both would have been silent bugs.

  • "The result is negated (thrust opposes the facing direction)." It is not. The sequence after the shifts is AND #$01 / EOR #$FF / ADC #$01, which sign-extends the integer byte that the rotates have shifted the sign out of, and the accumulate is ADC. The table already points the way the ship flies: heading 0 is up and its Y entry is −2.5. Negating it drives the ship backwards.
  • "Rotation rate: 3 out of 4 frames, ~8.4 steps/second at 50fps." Three in four of 50 is 37.5, and ship_input_rotate is exactly AND #$03 / BEQ return. A full turn takes a little under a second.
Measurement

The cave costs tens of fills a frame, not hundreds

The terrain is drawn as two solid masses with a gap. Drawn naively — one horizontal line per scanline per wall — a 240-row screen costs 240 draws a frame, which would be marginal. But the RLE the original uses is mostly long runs at a constant X, so consecutive scanlines share a wall position and collapse into one rectangle. Decoding all six levels and sliding a 120-scanline window down each of them gives the real cost. The top two thirds of every level is open sky at a constant X — one fill, and the player never flies up there — so "typical" below is the median taken over the cave alone:

So a typical frame is 13 to 56 BOX calls and the pathological frame — a long diagonal on level 5, where every scanline genuinely does differ — is 158. That is the number to size the budget against, and it is comfortably inside 33 ms if a BOX costs anything like what Chuckie Egg's drawing did. Spike 1 confirms it on the board before phase 3 starts.

Two consequences worth noticing. First, the whole bidirectional RLE decoder — four decoder triples, a circular window, forward and reverse step functions — exists only to fit 256 bytes. We decode each level once at load time into two arrays and throw all of it away. Second, because we hold the entire wall profile, terrain collision becomes a handful of array lookups instead of a side effect of XOR plotting.

Decisions

Architecture

Coordinates: work in BBC world units throughout

The BBC's terrain unit is a byte column (4 pixels) wide and, at its half-resolution rendering, 2 pixel rows tall, on a display whose pixels are about 1.07× wider than tall. Keep the game's arithmetic in those units and map x_px = 4 × world_x, y_px = 2 × world_y and the original geometry comes back within 7%. This matters more than it sounds: the angle-to-force tables are deliberately elliptical (Y range ~5.0 against X ~2.5) to compensate for MODE 1's pixel aspect. Take those tables verbatim and the compensation is already correct. Substituting SIN/COS would throw it away.

Screen layout

The BBC play area is 288 × 146 pixels — a 2.1:1 letterbox once pixel aspect is applied. Our 320 × 240 square-pixel screen is 1.33:1, so a full-screen playfield would show roughly half as much again vertically and make the game easier. Recommendation: 320 × 176 playfield with a 64-row panel beneath it for fuel, score, lives and the shield indicator. That is 80 × 88 world units against the BBC's 72 × 73 — 11% wider and 20% taller. Tunable, and worth playing both before fixing.

Timing: a 50 Hz simulation under a 30 Hz render

The original integrates position every frame at 50 Hz and updates forces on 6 ticks in 16. Both cadences are part of the feel, so decouple them from our frame rate: run a fixed 50 Hz simulation step off an accumulator (1 or 2 steps per rendered frame) and render at the 33 ms cadence we already pace at. Do not rescale the constants to 30 Hz — the tick gating pattern is irregular ($00, $03, $05, $08, $0B, $0D) and would not survive it.

Terrain rendering: run-merged solid fills, no scrolling primitive

SPRITE SCROLL is tempting but wrong here. It helps only when the exposed edge is cheap to fill, and Thrust's camera moves diagonally, so a 1-pixel horizontal scroll exposes a full-height strip that needs the same per-scanline work as a complete redraw. Redraw the visible window each frame from the decoded wall arrays, merging runs into BOX calls, into the framebuffer, then one FRAMEBUFFER COPY. This is the same pattern Chuckie Egg already runs at 30 fps, and the measurement above says it fits.

Ship: 17 pre-rotated sprites and the mirror bit

Generate all 17 shapes at build time from the disassembly's sprite data — its single-stream format, where $81-$FE encodes "end of row" and "first pixel of the next row" in one byte, is straightforward to decode in Python. Headings 17-31 are the mirror of 32−a, which the SPRITE SHOW rotation parameter gives us for free. Same gen_sprites.py pipeline as Chuckie Egg.

Objects as sprites, particles as pixels

Up to 32 objects per level (the table above shows 4 to 19 in practice) as sprite buffers, well inside MAXBLITBUF. The 32 particle slots — player bullets, hostile bullets, stars, debris — are single pixels; draw them straight into the framebuffer with PIXEL rather than paying sprite bookkeeping for them.

Collision: cheaper and better than the original

We cannot get collision free from XOR, and we do not want to. Ship against terrain becomes six or eight hull points tested against wallL() and wallR() at the ship's own scanlines — a handful of comparisons, and more robust than the original, which could only notice a collision on the frame after it happened. Bullets and objects use the sprite engine's collision or plain AABB.

Audio

Nine sound blocks, straight across

As with Chuckie Egg, the OSWORD &07 blocks are already in PLAY BBC SOUND's parameter order — channel word, amplitude, pitch, duration — so they transcribe rather than translate. Amplitudes 1-4 select the four envelopes; negative amplitudes are plain volumes.

The envelopes decode the same way. Envelope 1, the gun, is PLAY BBC ENVELOPE 1, 2, -5, -3, -5, 2, 3, 50, 126, -7, -7, -12, 126, 0; envelope 3, the explosion, is PLAY BBC ENVELOPE 3, 4, 0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 126, -4, -2, -4, 126, 110; envelope 4, the hostile gun, is PLAY BBC ENVELOPE 4, 1, -1, -1, -1, 18, 18, 18, 50, -12, -12, -12, 110, 70.

Channel 0 is the noise source and channel 1 sets its pitch when noise pitch 3 or 7 is used. Thrust's explosion depends on that pairing, and channel 1 is otherwise unused in the game — so leave it to the explosion and put anything we add elsewhere.
Plan

Phases

0Spikes

Answer the three questions that change the design

A day. Nothing here ships.

  • S1 — fill rate. Passed Measured on the PC3 by flying the camera down all six levels a scanline at a time: 9.0 to 12.2 ms average and 20 ms in the worst frame, including the FRAMEBUFFER COPY. Standalone that is 82 to 111 fps. The striped half-resolution fallback is not needed.
  • S2 — entity cost. Next Time one sprite plus 19 object sprites plus 32 PIXEL particles moved and drawn. Accept if under 8 ms.
  • S3 — the letterbox. Put a static cave on screen at 320×176 and at full 320×240 and decide which reads as Thrust.
1Data

Extract terrain, sprites, objects and sound

Done Mirrors chuckie_tools/.

gen_terrain.py decodes the six RLE levels to wall arrays; gen_sprites.py pulls the 17 ship headings, the pod, the shield and the nine object shapes; gen_objects.py reads the per-level object tables; gen_sound.py emits the envelopes and blocks as PLAY BBC. All four write into out/, and all but the last draw what they extracted.

The check that turned out to be worth having is not a screenshot comparison but an arithmetic one. gen_objects.py reports what fraction of each object stands inside rock. Across all six levels every one of the 33 limpet guns comes out at exactly 0.25 buried, every one of the 19 fuel cells at 0.30, every pod and door switch at 0.00 and every reactor at 0.10. Those figures are identical level to level because the designer placed objects against the wall, so they can only come out clean if the terrain decoder and the object tables agree to the column. They did not at first: the right wall starts at $FF rather than 0 and segment 0 of the RLE is never read, and getting that wrong put every cave 255 scanlines below its own objects.

2Terrain

A cave you can scroll around with the cursor keys

Done No physics. The renderer, standalone.

Wall arrays in, camera position in, run-merged fills out. Bas/thrust.bas decodes a level's RLE at load, draws the cave, and flies the camera about with the arrow keys; N and P change level and S writes a screenshot.

Drawing the cave beats drawing the walls. The plan assumed two fills per run, one for each wall. Filling the whole playfield with rock and then punching the cave out of it in black is strictly cheaper, because the two walls rarely change on the same scanline and a run only breaks when either does: the worst frame drops from 149 boxes to 112, and the typical frame by a quarter. It also replaces "clear, then draw two walls" with "one fill, then one box per run".

3Flight

The ship, gravity, thrust, and hitting the walls

Done The whole game's soul.

The force pipeline is in the original's order — rotate, integrate, derive position, compute next frame's force — with its constants: gravity per level, the angle tables verbatim, thrust over 16, X drag 63/64 and Y drag 255/256, the six active ticks in sixteen, rotation on three frames in four. The simulation runs at a fixed 50 Hz off an accumulator under the 30 Hz draw, so the irregular tick pattern survives. Collision is eight hull points against the wall arrays.

Checked by driving SimStep from the board's command prompt rather than by eye: 48 steps of full thrust from rest in open sky, at each of the four cardinal headings.

HeadingΔyΔxExpected

Up and down are symmetric to the second decimal about free fall, left and right are exact mirrors, and fuel came off at 18 units in 48 steps — six in sixteen, as the tick gate says. The vertical response is 2.16× the horizontal, which is the table's 2:1 ellipse plus X drag being four times stronger: that asymmetry is the feel of Thrust, and it comes out of the data without being asked for.

Still to come here: the exhaust flame, the explosion animation, and a proper look at whether the ship turns at the right rate to fly by.

4Pod

The tractor beam and the pendulum

The part everyone remembers.

Two-stage attachment on Manhattan distance ($75 to start the beam, $84 to lock). Midpoint as the average of the two positions, force vector halved for the doubled mass, initial angular velocity from the cross product of force and displacement. The 7-level binary search for the starting tether angle can be replaced by ATAN2(y, x) — one of the few places where having a maths library is simply better.

5World

Fuel cells, limpet guns, bullets, the reactor

Objects and particles.

Object table, the tractor beam on fuel cells, the guns that track and fire, bullets inheriting ship velocity, the reactor, shooting it, and the ten-second escape with the countdown sound. Debris explosions from the particle pool.

6Sound

Four envelopes and nine blocks

A short phase, now that the engine exists.

Chuckie Egg's conversion took an afternoon once the numbers were in hand. Engine noise retriggered while thrusting is the only one needing per-frame attention.

7Game

Missions, score, and the difficulty cycle

Structure around the simulation.

level_number = (mission_number - 1) MOD 6 — six terrains cycling with escalating difficulty, which is the same shape as Chuckie Egg's five cycles and can reuse that structure. Then reversed gravity and the invisible landscape on later missions, the status panel, the title screen and the high score.

Risks

What could go wrong

RiskLikelihoodMitigation
Terrain redraw does not fit the frameClosedMeasured on the board in phase 2: 20 ms in the worst frame of the worst level, 9 to 12 ms typically, copy included. Thirteen milliseconds of the budget are left for everything else, which is what S2 has to fit into.
64 moving things against Chuckie Egg's nineMediumParticles are single pixels, not sprites. The 32-slot pool is rarely full — it peaks only during debris explosions, which are also the moments when a dropped frame is least visible.
The flight model does not feel like ThrustMediumThe likeliest cause would be substituting trigonometry for the elliptical angle tables, or rescaling the tick gating to 30 Hz. Both are explicitly ruled out above. Phase 3 exists to catch it early.
Letterbox versus full screen changes difficultyCertainIt is a design decision, not a bug. Spike S3 makes it deliberately.
Sprite data transcription errorsLowRender every extracted shape to PNG and eyeball it, as with Chuckie Egg's hens.
